---
title: "Septuagint"
tags: [concept]
---

The Septuagint (often abbreviated as LXX) is the earliest extant Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, along with several additional texts not found in the Hebrew canon (the Apocrypha/Deuterocanonicals). The translation process began in Alexandria, Egypt, in the 3rd century BCE, initially to serve the large population of Hellenized Jews who spoke Greek rather than Hebrew or Aramaic. The name "Septuagint" derives from the tradition that 70 (or 72) Jewish scholars independently produced identical translations of the Torah.

In the context of the *Lord of Spirits* and early Christian history, the Septuagint is of paramount importance. It was the primary Bible of the early Church and the version most frequently quoted by the New Testament authors, including the Apostle Paul. The Septuagint's translation choices deeply influenced Christian theological vocabulary; for instance, the Greek word *kyrios* (Lord) was used to translate the divine name Yahweh, a usage directly applied to Jesus Christ in the New Testament.

For the Orthodox Church, the Septuagint is the authoritative and canonical Old Testament text. It is preferred over the later Masoretic Text (the standard Hebrew text) because it preserves ancient textual traditions that predate the Masoretic standardization and because it was the Scriptures universally used and preserved by the early Church Fathers. The inclusion of the Deuterocanonical books in the Septuagint also reflects the broader canon accepted by ancient Christianity.
