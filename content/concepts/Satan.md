---
title: "Satan"
tags: [concept]
---

In the biblical and ancient Near Eastern context, "satan" (Hebrew: *ha-satan*) originally functioned more as a title or a role than a personal name. It translates to "the adversary" or "the accuser." In the Old Testament, such as in the Book of Job or Zechariah, *ha-satan* is depicted as a member of the Divine Council who acts as a prosecuting attorney against humanity. Over time, particularly during the Second Temple period, the term became associated with the specific, primary leader of the fallen spiritual powers.

The *Lord of Spirits* podcast heavily emphasizes the Orthodox understanding of Satan not as God's equal opposite (which would be dualism), but as a created, rebellious angelic being. Satan's fall was driven by pride and a desire to usurp God's authority, leading to his casting out from the heavenly realms. He is fundamentally a deceiver and the "prince of this world," who sought to enslave humanity through the fear of death and the temptation to sin, essentially holding humanity hostage.

In Orthodox theology, Christ's incarnation, crucifixion, and harrowing of Hades represent the ultimate defeat of Satan. The cross, seemingly a victory for the devil, was actually the means by which he was overthrown; as St. John Chrysostom describes it, Hades swallowed a mortal body but encountered God. While Satan continues to wage spiritual warfare against the Church, his power is fundamentally broken, and his ultimate destruction in the Lake of Fire is assured at the Last Judgment.
