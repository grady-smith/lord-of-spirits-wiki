---
title: "Hellenism"
tags: [concept]
---
Hellenism refers to the spread and adoption of Greek culture, language, philosophy, and religion across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean following the conquests of Alexander the Great. This cultural synthesis deeply impacted the sociopolitical and religious landscape of Judea, introducing gymnasiums, Greek philosophical categories, and syncretic religious practices.

In the context of Second Temple Judaism, Hellenism provoked a profound crisis of identity. The Maccabean Revolt was largely a violent reaction against forced Hellenization by the Seleucid Empire, which sought to eradicate traditional Jewish Torah observance and impose the worship of Greek deities. The tension between assimilation and distinct covenantal faithfulness shaped the diverse Jewish factions (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes) of the 1st century.

For Orthodox theology, Hellenism provides the linguistic and philosophical vocabulary through which the early Church articulated its doctrines. While the Apostles and Church Fathers fiercely rejected Greek paganism and polytheism—often identifying the Greco-Roman gods as demonic entities—they utilized Greek philosophical terms (like *logos* and *ousia*) to precisely define the mysteries of the Incarnation and the Holy Trinity, effectively "baptizing" Hellenistic thought into the service of the Gospel.
