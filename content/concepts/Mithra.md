Mithra (or Mithras) was a prominent deity in ancient Persian religion who was later adapted into the Greco-Roman world as the center of the Mithraic Mysteries, a secret mystery cult highly popular among the Roman military from the 1st to 4th centuries AD. Originally associated with light, truth, and covenants, the Roman iteration of Mithras was worshipped in underground temples (mithraea) and characterized by the iconic tauroctony—the ritual slaying of a bull.

In the *Lord of Spirits* podcast, mystery cults like that of Mithras are discussed as paradigms of pagan religious life where initiates sought communion with and protection from specific spiritual entities. Unlike modern conceptualizations of religion, these cults were transactional and experiential, demanding ritual purity, sacrifice, and secret knowledge to bind the practitioner to the patron deity, which Orthodox theology identifies as a demonic spirit.

The early Christians strongly opposed the Mithraic cult, recognizing its rituals—which included communal meals and initiation rites—as demonic counterfeits to the Christian sacraments. The Christian mysteries (sacraments) were understood as the true participation in the Divine Council through Christ, rendering the blood-soaked, secretive rites of Mithras not only obsolete but actively hostile to the kingdom of God.
