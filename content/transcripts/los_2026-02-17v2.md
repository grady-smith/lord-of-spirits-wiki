## Why "Latin" not "Western" Theology

[[Fr. Stephen]] begins by clarifying why he uses the term "Latin Theology" instead of the more common "Western Theology." He argues that "The West" is a misleading and artificial construct.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=07:01:93)

He points out that the definition of "The West" arbitrarily includes and excludes cultures and periods to fit a preferred narrative. For example, ancient Greece is claimed for its philosophy and democracy, but modern Greece is often excluded. Russia is sometimes included and sometimes not, depending on the context. Applying such a shifting definition to theology becomes confusing.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=08:31:69)

In contrast, [[Fr. Stephen]] finds "Latin Theology" to be a more precise and useful descriptor. This term accurately traces a lineage that begins with the translation of Greek Christian concepts into the Latin language, a process that shaped the theology of Western Europe and pre-Islamic North Africa. This linguistic and conceptual framework extends through [[Roman Catholicism]] (whose liturgy was in Latin until Vatican II) and even into [[Protestantism]], which inherited its core theological vocabulary (e.g., justification, sanctification) from Latin.

## Translation and Conceptual Drift

The development of Latin theology begins with a process of translation, not just of texts like the Bible and the [[Liturgy]], but of theological concepts themselves.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=13:26:42)

This same process occurred as Christian thought moved from the Greek-speaking world into the Latin-speaking world. The transition began in the late 2nd century when the need for Latin texts grew in the West. By the late 4th and early 5th centuries, Latin had become dominant in the Western Church, diminishing the need for clergy and laity to learn Greek.

A critical problem arises from this linguistic shift: conceptual drift. While the first generation of translators understood the original Greek concepts they were labeling with Latin words, subsequent generations lost direct access to the Greek. They were left only with the Latin terms, whose meanings could and did shift over time, sometimes significantly departing from the original concept.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=19:24:62)

The Fathers provide several key examples of this conceptual slippage between Greek and Latin terminology.

### Worship vs. Veneration

In Greek, the distinction is made between *latria* (the service and worship due to [[God]] alone) and *proskyneo* (the act of veneration, literally "to bend the knee," which can be offered to saints, icons, or respected persons). In Latin, *adoramus* (from which we get "adoration," etymologically meaning "to pray to") was used for worship, while *veneratio* was used for veneration. The issue is that the very word for worship in Latin could be misunderstood, as it simply meant "to pray to," an act that can also be directed toward saints. This linguistic ambiguity contributed to later confusion and debates.

### The [[Trinity]]

The doctrine of the [[Trinity]] provides a stark example. The Greek formula is one *Ousia* (essence) and three *Hypostases* (persons). The Latin formula is one *Substantia* and three *Personae*.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=32:09:12)

Despite the identical etymology, the terms were used in opposite ways. *Substantia* in Latin corresponds to the Greek *Ousia*, referring to [[God]]'s *oneness*. Conversely, *Hypostasis* in Greek refers to the *threeness* of the Persons. This demonstrates how even direct translations can lead to different conceptual applications in theology.

### Divine Energies

The Aristotelian distinction between *dynamis* (potentiality) and *energeia* (actuality/activity) is foundational to the Orthodox understanding of [[God]]'s energies. *Energeia* refers to [[God]] Himself present and acting in the world. However, when this was translated into Latin, *potentia* and *actus* were used. *Actus* came to mean an "effect" or "act" rather than [[God]]'s direct presence in action. This shift laid the groundwork for the later Scholastic concept of [[God]] as *Actus Purus* (Pure Act), a being who is changeless and therefore not involved in "activity," a departure from the Orthodox concept of the uncreated energies.

## The Influence of St. Augustine and Scholasticism

St. Augustine of Hippo is the pivotal figure in framing Latin theological terminology. While he was a saint who understood Christian concepts, his limited knowledge of Greek meant he had to find Latin equivalents, which later generations would interpret based on the evolving meanings of those Latin words, not the original Greek concepts. He also integrated Platonic metaphysics into his theology, introducing ideas like divine simplicity that would have a long-lasting impact.

[[Fr. Stephen]] notes that St. Augustine himself reconsidered and withdrew many of his earlier ideas in his book *Retractions*, particularly concerning his anti-Pelagian writings which heavily influenced later figures like [[John]] Calvin. He argues that Augustine's actual view on original sin was one of privation (*reatus*, a liability or lack) rather than legal guilt (*culpa*), a distinction lost on many of his successors.

The period following Augustine saw the rise of Scholasticism, with figures like Peter Lombard systematizing theology. Lombard's *Sentences* introduced the critical distinction between the **sufficiency** of [[Christ]]'s work (enough to save everyone) and its **efficiency** (only applied to some). This framework led directly to the development of the theology of **merit**.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=1:28:45)

This system evolved to include concepts like:
- **Condign Merit:** What [[God]] "owes" a person because He promised it (e.g., forgiveness through baptism).
- **Congruent Merit:** Grace that is "fitting" for [[God]] to grant in response to good works.
- **Temporal vs. Eternal Guilt:** Leading to the idea of Purgatory.
- **The Treasury of Merit:** The excess merit of [[Christ]] and the saints that the Pope could dispense, which became the basis for indulgences.

## The Reformation and Its Consequences

The Protestant Reformation, rather than returning to an earlier form of Christianity, largely reacted against the excesses of medieval Latin theology while remaining within its conceptual framework. Martin Luther's solution to the anxiety of merit was to make the transaction instantaneous and final.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=1:56:16)

This doctrine of "double imputation" (our sin imputed to [[Christ]], His righteousness imputed to us) was an extrinsic, one-time transactional event. However, this didn't solve the problem of assurance, as people began to doubt whether their belief was sincere enough, leading to cycles of "getting saved again."

The fragmentation that began with the Reformation accelerated in America. The First Great Awakening was largely Puritan and [[Calvinist]]. The Second Great Awakening was revivalist, emotionally-driven, and anti-intellectual, leading to the formation of new religious movements like Mormonism. This eventually created the modern American religious landscape, with a "mainline" [[Protestantism]] that drifted into liberalism and an "Evangelicalism" that became a broad, politically-defined coalition rather than a theologically unified movement.

## Modern [[Roman Catholicism]] and the Orthodox Alternative

[[Fr. Stephen]] argues that modern [[Roman Catholicism]] has itself been "Protestantized." In Europe, this took the form of "Hegelization," influenced by philosophers who saw doctrine as evolving. Theologians like Karl Rahner, with his "transcendental Thomism," argued that the modern church understands Christianity better than even [[St. Paul]] did. This view was influential at Vatican II, leading to significant changes in practice and self-understanding. In the United States, [[Roman Catholicism]] has become increasingly "evangelicalized," aligning itself with the political and cultural concerns of American Evangelicalism.

In stark contrast to this history of development, change, and fragmentation, the [[Orthodox Church]] stands for **preservationism**.

**[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2026-02-17v2.mp3#t=2:30:17)

The episode concludes by positioning the [[Orthodox Church]] as a "safe haven" from the long, winding trajectory of Latin theology, which has led to its current state of confusion and division. It offers a spiritual life and a connection to the historic faith that is not based on evolving human systems but on preserving the apostolic deposit.
