## "[[Son of Man]]" in Hebrew and Greek

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-12-09.mp3#t=09:33)
**[[Fr. Stephen]]:** There are two ways that "son of man" occurs, if you want to be woodenly literal. There are cases that could literally be translated "son of a man" and there are cases that could be literally translated "son of Adam." In both Hebrew and in Greek, there are two different words that are translated "man." One of which sort of means "a man" as in a male person, and one of which refers to "man" like humanity or mankind. In Hebrew, the word for sort of "a man," a male human, is *ish*. And the word for humanity is *Adama*, and you can see the name Adam in there. In Greek, it's *aner* is sort of "a man" and *anthropos* is more "human," right, talking about humanity as such.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-12-09.mp3#t=13:26)
**[[Fr. Stephen]]:** What we find in the Hebrew and in the Greek of the Old Testament is that you will have examples of effectively a phrase like *Ben Ish*, right, son of a man, and *Ben Adama*, son of man like mankind. And you find in the Greek places that are *huios aner*, son of a man, and *huios anthropos*, son of mankind. But here's the thing, they don't match up. The Greek translators don't consistently translate *Ben Ish* as *huios aner*. They understood them to be synonyms in Hebrew and then they used them as synonyms in Greek.

## The Rabbinic Response

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-12-09.mp3#t=1:43:35)
**[[Fr. Stephen]]:** In the second century, that's when Christians are expelled from the synagogue. The whole idea that there are two powers in heaven is labeled a heresy in the modern sense. This is a borderline, a dividing line. If you believe in this, you're going to be expelled from the synagogue and treated as a Christian. And so they also decided that letting that literature proliferate in the first place was a mistake. So there was no writing. Things aren't written down. Everything is taught orally and by memorization in the rabbinical schools. But there are two places in two different tractates that directly address this whole issue of two powers in heaven.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-12-09.mp3#t=1:45:25)
**[[Fr. Stephen]]:** One of the two places... is Tractate Hagigah. And this story about this fellow Aher. Aher means "backwards." This is what they call him. He is like their prototypal heretic, the way Arius is sort of the prototypal Christian heretic. He has this vision. He's doing his sort of Merkabah prayers, where he's hoping to have this heavenly ascent. And at the end of this heavenly ascent, he sees the Archangel Metatron, and he sees him sitting down... So he concludes, "Oh, look, this is the second power in heaven." But Tractate Hagigah wants to make it clear that, as usual, Aher was wrong. So, in this story, the Archangel Metatron gets in trouble... he gets drugged off and whipped.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-12-09.mp3#t=1:49:53)
**[[Fr. Stephen]]:** The second place in the Talmud is in Tractate Sanhedrin. And this is with Rabbi Akiva, who is the most prominent rabbi of the early second century... He says, "Well, the two thrones," he's talking about the thrones in [[Daniel]] 7, "one is for God, one is for [[Yahweh]] the God of Israel, and one is for David, meaning one is for the Messiah." That would be a typical two powers in heaven view, which is probably what Rabbi Akiva actually believed. But he gets corrected in Tractate Sanhedrin, that no, no, one is for justice and one is for mercy. Meaning God sort of manifests himself as a young man when he's going to issue justice... and he appears as an old man when he's going to give mercy.
