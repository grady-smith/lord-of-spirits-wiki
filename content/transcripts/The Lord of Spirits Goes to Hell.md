# The Lord of Spirits Goes to Hell

## Introduction and Recap

[[Fr. Andrew]] Stephen Damick begins the episode by stating its title: [The Lord of Spirits Goes to Hell](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:02:07). This episode is presented as a Holy Week and Pascha special for the [[Orthodox]] Church. The previous two episodes focused on [[Sacred Geography]], discussing Paradise as the mountain of [[God]] and the underworld. This episode explores what happens when the Lord of Spirits, the incarnate Son of [[God]], [[Jesus]] [[Christ]], journeys into the underworld.

## Part 1: Baal Goes to Hell

### The Baal Cycle of Ugarit

[[Fr. Stephen]] De Young introduces the ancient city of [[Ugarit]], located in modern-day northwestern Syria. Discovered in the mid-20th century, the ruins of Ugarit yielded a vast library of clay tablets written in Ugaritic, a Northwest Semitic language closely related to Hebrew. [These texts provide invaluable context for understanding the Old Testament](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:07:01), including clarifying the meaning of previously obscure Hebrew words.

Among the most important discoveries was a series of tablets known as the **Baal Cycle**. [[Fr. Stephen]] explains that "Baal" is both a name and a title meaning "lord" or "master." The Baal Cycle is an epic poem that details the insurrection of the [[god]] Baal. [The story's arc is as follows](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:16:07):
1.  **Insurrection:** Baal rebels against the reigning gods, Yam (the sea) and Nahar (the river), to establish his father, El, as the most high [[god]].
2.  **Victory:** Baal defeats Yam and Nahar.
3.  **Descent to the Underworld:** After his victory, Baal decides to go to the underworld ([[Sheol]]).
4.  **Battle with Mot:** In the underworld, Baal fights Mot, the [[god]] of death. He is victorious but decides to remain in [[Sheol]].

This narrative of a victorious [[god]] descending into the realm of death provides a crucial pagan parallel that the scriptures directly address and subvert.

### Psalm 23 (24) as a Response to the Baal Cycle

[[Fr. Stephen]] argues that [[Psalm 23 (24)]] is a direct liturgical and theological response to the Baal Cycle.

- **Verses 1-2:** "The earth is [[Yahweh]]'s and the fullness thereof... for he has founded it upon the seas (Yam) and established it upon the floods (Nahar)." [[Fr. Stephen]] notes that this is a direct polemic. [It asserts that [[Yahweh]], not Baal, is the true Lord who has dominion over the chaotic waters of Yam and Nahar, having built creation on top of them](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:27:00). This verse is used in the [[Orthodox]] funeral service to claim the deceased for [[God]], not for the lord of the underworld.

- **Verses 3-6:** "Who shall ascend the mountain of the Lord?" This is a rhetorical question aimed at Baal, who famously ascended Mount Zaphon. The Psalm's answer is not Baal, but the righteous man with "clean hands and a pure heart." [This contrasts the pagan deity with the faithful human who can truly approach God](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:29:21).

- **Verses 7-10:** "Lift up your heads, O ye gates... and the King of glory shall come in." [[Fr. Stephen]] reveals that this is a direct quotation and subversion of the Baal Cycle. In the pagan epic, Baal shouts to the other gods, ["Lift up your heads, O ye gods,"](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:35:07) to rally them. The Psalm takes these exact words and places them in the mouth of [[Yahweh]], who is not addressing other gods, but the very gates of [[Hades]] as He prepares to invade it. This is a depiction of [[The Harrowing of Hades]].

[[Fr. Stephen]] connects this to the [[Orthodox]] Paschal service, where the [[priest]], standing outside the darkened church, bangs on the doors and recites these verses. [The church doors represent the gates of [[Hades]], and the [[priest]] acts in the person of [[Christ]], breaking them down to bring the light of the [[resurrection]] into the darkness](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=00:38:15). This liturgical act preserves the ancient context of the Psalm, demonstrating how Holy Tradition maintained the scripture's original meaning even when the historical evidence from Ugarit was lost for 3,000 years.

## Part 2: The Song of the Sea

### The Crossing of the Reed Sea (Yam Suph)

[[Fr. Stephen]] shifts the focus to [[Exodus 15]], the Song of the Sea, which is sung after Israel passes through the parted waters.

- **The Red Sea vs. The Reed Sea:** The term "Red Sea" comes from the Greek translation ([[Septuagint]]). The original Hebrew is *Yam Suph*. While this is often translated as "Sea of Reeds," an even older meaning of *Suph* in Egyptian is "end," "extremity," or "border." [Thus, *Yam Suph* is the "border sea" that marked the eastern edge of Egypt](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=01:03:50).

- **The Underworld Connection:** Critically, in Egyptian religion, the "Sea of Reeds" was also a place in the underworld. It was the destination for the damned, known as "the drowned ones" (*Mkeyu*). When Pharaoh and his army are drowned in the *Yam Suph*, [they are literally being sent to the Egyptian hell, becoming the very "drowned ones" of their own mythology](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=01:04:30).

- **[[Passover]] and [[Baptism]]:** In stark contrast, Israel passes *through* this realm of death and emerges safely on the other side into new life. This is the central event of the [[Passover]]. This act of passing through the waters of death into life is precisely why [[St. Paul]] connects this event to [[Christian]] [[baptism]].

### Smack Talk Against Egyptian Gods

The Song of the Sea, like Psalm 23 (24), is filled with polemics against pagan gods.

- **The Right Hand and Strong Arm of [[Yahweh]]:** The song repeatedly refers to [[Yahweh]]'s "right hand" and "strong arm" achieving the victory (e.g., [[Exodus 15:6]]). This is a direct challenge to Egyptian theology. [The Egyptian [[god]] of war, Montu, was known as the "right arm" of Ra, and a primary title for Pharaoh was "Horus of the strong arm."](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=01:10:50) The song declares that [[Yahweh]]'s arm, not Pharaoh's or Montu's, is the truly powerful one. Centuries later, the [[prophet]] [[Ezekiel]] would echo this, saying [[Yahweh]] had "broken the arm of Pharaoh" ([[Ezekiel 30:21]]).

- **Debir and Keteb, the Servants of Mot:** The discussion returns to the Canaanite [[god]] of death, Mot. Mot had two servants, Debir and Keteb, whose names translate to "pestilence" and "plague." They were seen as the agents of death in the world. However, scriptures like [[Habakkuk 3]] and [[Psalm 90 (91)]] re-appropriate these figures, portraying them not as servants of Mot, but as agents under [[Yahweh]]'s ultimate command. The "Noonday Demon" mentioned in Psalm 90 (91) is a direct reference to Keteb.

This consistent theme of disarming pagan deities and re-asserting [[Yahweh]]'s supreme authority is central to the Old Testament's theological message. [[Christ]]'s descent into [[Hades]] is the ultimate fulfillment of this pattern, where He doesn't just defeat a local deity but dismantles the power of Death itself.

## Part 3: [[Christ]] Goes to Hell

### The [[Gospel of Nicodemus]]

To conclude, [[Fr. Andrew]] reads a powerful and dramatic passage from the [[Gospel of Nicodemus]], an ancient [[Christian]] text dating to around the 4th century. While not canonical scripture, it has been deeply influential on [[Orthodox]] iconography and hymnography of the [[Resurrection]].

The passage describes the events in [[Hades]] from the perspective of Hell and [[Satan]].
- **Dialogue between Hell and [[Satan]]:** [[Satan]] announces he is bringing [[Jesus]] to be imprisoned. Hell expresses terror, recalling how [[Jesus]] had previously raised Lazarus from the dead, robbing him of a soul. [Hell says, "I adjure thee by the strength of thine and mine own, that thou bring him not unto me."](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=01:29:42)

- **The Voice of Thunder:** A voice like thunder cries out, "Remove, O princes, your gates and be ye lift up, ye everlasting doors, and the King of glory shall come in." This is the quote from Psalm 23 (24). Hell asks, "Who is this King of glory?" and [[David]], from among the dead, replies with the rest of the Psalm, identifying the King as "The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle... the Lord of hosts."

- **[[Christ]]'s Arrival:** The King of glory, [[Jesus]] [[Christ]], appears in the form of a man, and a great light fills the darkness of [[Hades]]. [He tramples upon Death, seizes [[Satan]], and delivers him to the power of Hell to be held until the Second Coming](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2021-04-23.mp3#t=01:35:23).

- **Liberation of the [[Saints]]:** [[Christ]] then draws Adam to Himself and leads all the righteous dead out of [[Hades]], fulfilling the promise of salvation. The [[demons]] cry out in terror, recognizing [[Jesus]] as the one who has undone their power and brought life into their realm of death.

This vivid depiction captures the core of the [[Orthodox]] understanding of [[The Harrowing of Hades]]: not just a rescue mission, but a triumphant invasion, a cosmic battle, and the ultimate victory of life over death, light over darkness, and [[God]] over the [[devil]].
