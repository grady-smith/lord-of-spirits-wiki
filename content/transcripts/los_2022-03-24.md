## Question 1: The Meaning of "[[Liturgy]]"

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=03:28:00)

Christopher from Danbury, Connecticut, asks about the meaning of the word "liturgy." He knows it does not mean "the work of the people" and asks if a better understanding would be "work *for* the people," as in [[God]] doing the work for us.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=04:55:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that the Greek word [[Liturgy - Divine Service|leitourgia]] originally meant "public works" in a pre-Christian context. In the ancient Greek city-state (polis), citizens were expected to fund public festivals, choruses, and other civic projects as a service to the community. Aristotle is the first known person to use a phrase like "divine liturgy," referring to the duty of landowners to set aside land for building temples. This was both a public work and a religious activity, as the gods were part of the community.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=07:25:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] further explains that this term was later used in the [[Septuagint]] to translate Hebrew verbs related to the service of the Levites in the [[Tabernacle]]. This was seen as their responsibility within the covenant with [[God]]. The early Christians adopted this term because they saw themselves as a people (a new polis) whose public work was Christian worship.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=09:33:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] clarifies that the term was translated into Latin in two ways: *ministerium* (where we get "ministry") and *officium* ("office" or "duty"). This is why government departments in Great Britain are called "ministries" and why church services are sometimes called "offices."

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=11:23:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] concludes that the relationship is instrumental. The clergy and the people are like an axe used to chop down a tree. While you can say the axe chopped the tree, it was the agent using the axe that was the true actor. Similarly, in the liturgy, [[God]] is doing the work instrumentally through the clergy and the people.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=11:48:00)

[[Fr. Andrew]] asks about the deacon's exclamation at the beginning of the liturgy, which is sometimes translated as "It is time for the Lord to act," suggesting [[God]] is the primary actor. [[Fr. Stephen]] clarifies that while that translation "sounds much cooler," the more accurate translation is, "It is time to begin the service of the Lord." The former translation requires some "special pleading" with Greek usage.

## Question 2: Mental Illness and Demonic Influence

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=15:35:00)

Ben from Grand Rapids, Michigan, who is studying mental health, asks about a professor's claim that until about 200 years ago, no cultures distinguished mental health issues from demonic possession or supernatural influence. He asks if there has ever been a time in Christian thought where a distinction was drawn between the two, especially since some mental disorders can be treated with medication.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=17:17:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that the modern mindset creates a false "either/or" dichotomy. Pre-modern people, including the authors of Scripture, sought to show the *connection* between the spiritual, mental, and physical, not separate them. In the Gospels, [[Christ]]'s healing ministry combines casting out demons, healing physical ailments, and forgiving sins, showing their interconnectedness. This is not the same as the "crass" view of [[Job]]'s friends, who believed suffering was a direct punishment for a specific sin.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=20:17:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] rejects the "ghost in the machine" view of the soul and body. A human person is a unity. A person suffering from a debilitating physical ailment will also likely struggle with mental and spiritual issues. The spiritual, mental, and physical are never truly separate in our actual experience. The modern distinction is an artificial one that pre-modern people, including the Church Fathers, did not make. They had a more nuanced view where spiritual, physical, and mental causes and effects were all intertwined.

## Question 3: Cain's Wife and the "Sons of [[God]]"

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=22:47:00)

Cynthia asks a two-part question: 1) Who did Cain have children with? 2) Is this related to the giants in [[Genesis]] 6?

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=24:44:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] addresses the first part, stating that the Bible does not name Cain's wife. A literal reading suggests it must have been his sister. He then discusses and critiques "co-Adamism," the theory that there were other, pre-Adamic humans. This idea was popular in the 19th and early 20th centuries and was often used for racist purposes (e.g., to claim that only certain races descended from Adam).

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=26:45:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] points out the major theological problem with co-Adamism: it breaks the connection between Adam's sin and the entrance of death into the world. If there were other humans who lived and died before Adam's fall, then death is natural, which contradicts the entire theology of salvation presented in the New Testament, where [[Christ]] defeats death.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=24:30:00)

Regarding the second part of the question about [[Genesis]] 6, [[Fr. Stephen]] refers the caller back to previous episodes on giants, confirming that the topic of [[Nephilim - Giants]] is indeed what is being discussed in that passage.

## Question 4: The Nature of the Soul

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=29:12:00)

Emilian from Romania asks for clarification on the nature of the soul. If the soul is the "life of the body" and not an immortal "thing," how should we understand the afterlife, the demonization of [[Nephilim]] souls, and the Old Testament concept of the soul descending to the underworld but also returning to [[God]]?

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=31:37:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that the idea of an immortal soul comes from Plato, not the Bible. In Hebrew, *nephesh* (soul) just means "life." When [[God]] creates sea creatures, the text says the "souls that swim in the sea." Animals and even plants have souls (life), just of a different kind than human souls. In Latin, *anima* means soul, which is why we call a soulless object "inanimate."

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=33:10:00)

When the soul leaves the body, it means life has left the body. We get spatial language about "going down" to the underworld or "up" to heaven, but this is analogical, as a soul does not occupy physical space. The Bible gives us analogies for the intermediate state because we cannot fully comprehend it. The important thing is that our life continues, contingently, because it is created and preserved by [[God]]. This life will be reunited with the body at the resurrection. A demonized life, such as that of the [[Nephilim - Giants]], is one that becomes evil and functions in a demonic way.

## Question 5: Catechumens in the Narthex and "Death by Holiness"

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=36:35:00)

Evan asks about the ancient practice of catechumens and penitents standing in the narthex rather than the nave during the liturgy. He wonders if this is connected to the concept of "death by holiness," where the unprepared cannot draw near to [[God]]'s presence.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=37:51:00)

[[Fr. Andrew]] and [[Fr. Stephen]] affirm that this is a symbolic way of expressing that very concept. Those who have not yet undergone purification through baptism (the catechumens) or who are undergoing a period of separation due to sin (the penitents) are not yet ready to enter fully into the sacred space where [[Christ]] is present. The practice reflects the need for preparation to approach the Holy [[God]].

## Question 6: The Serpent's Seed

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=38:24:00)

Jesse asks about the meaning of the "serpent's seed" in [[Genesis 3]].

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=39:41:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that this is not about a literal, biological lineage from the serpent, a teaching associated with figures like William Branham and which has Gnostic roots. Instead, "sonship" in the Bible is primarily about image-bearing. [[Christ]] tells the Pharisees that they are "sons of your father the devil" because they bear his image and do his works. The "serpent's seed" refers to those who bear the image of the serpent, while the "woman's seed" refers to those who bear the image of [[God]], culminating in [[Christ]].

## Question 7: The Origin of the Synagogue

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=43:10:00)

Noah asks about the origins of the synagogue, noting its absence in much of the Old Testament and its role in relation to the [[Temple]].

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=44:03:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that the synagogue system arose during the Babylonian Exile and in the Jewish diaspora. Since the people were separated from the [[Temple]], they could no longer offer sacrifices. The synagogue became a gathering place for the community to read the Scriptures, sing [[Psalms]], pray, and maintain their identity as a people. It was a non-sacrificial form of worship. This model of worship and community gathering was then directly adopted by the early Christian church, forming the basis for the first part of the Divine [[Liturgy]] (the [[Liturgy]] of the Word).

## Question 8: The Role of Spontaneity in Orthodox Life

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=48:35:00)

Ben asks about the role of spontaneity in the Orthodox Christian life, contrasting it with the emphasis on it in his former charismatic/evangelical background, where it's seen as a sign of being led by the [[Holy Spirit]].

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=49:43:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] notes that the [[Holy Spirit]] is a spirit of order, not chaos. The idea that spontaneity equals authenticity is a modern one critiqued in Lori Branch's book, *Rituals of Spontaneity*. A distinction must be made between an unexpected, genuine experience of the [[Holy Spirit]] (like St. Elizabeth meeting the Theotokos) and the practice of "riffing off the top of your head" as a supposed sign of spirituality. Attributing one's own thoughts or erroneous ideas to the [[Holy Spirit]] is a serious matter, bordering on heresy or blasphemy because it attributes human failings to [[God]] Himself. True discernment is not about figuring out a secret "will of [[God]]" for every small decision but about making good, wise choices based on a life lived in [[Christ]].

## Question 9: Reading the Bible and the Theotokos as Daughter of [[God]]

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=52:47:00)

Svetlana asks two questions. First, her son, a seminarian in Russia, asks how modern people should read the Bible if it is neither purely literal history nor pure allegory. Second, she asks about the paradoxical titles of the [[Theotokos]] as both the Mother and Daughter of [[God]], and whether this logic could make [[Jesus]] his own son.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=59:02:00)

[[Fr. Stephen]] addresses the first question by saying it will be the topic of the next episode.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=59:08:00)

Regarding the second question, [[Fr. Andrew]] explains that these titles refer to different kinds of relationships. The Theotokos is the Daughter of [[God]] by grace and adoption, as all Christians are. She is the Mother of [[God]] by giving birth to the incarnate Son of [[God]]. These are not the same type of relationship, so the logic does not lead to a paradox where [[Jesus]] is his own son. [[Christ]]'s Sonship is unique and ontological, part of His very being within the Holy [[Trinity]], whereas the other relationships are based on grace, function, and adoption.

## Question 10: The Evil Eye and Curses

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=01:00:46)

Tyler asks about the Evil Eye: what it is, whether curses are real, and whether amulets like the hamsa are idolatrous.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=01:04:03)

[[Fr. Stephen]] traces the concept of the Evil Eye back to ancient Near Eastern texts, such as a Ugaritic curse tablet that invokes the eye of the goddess Anat. The core idea is that through envy or malice, one can direct a demonic power at someone else. This is a real spiritual danger. However, using amulets or other charms to ward it off is a form of folk magic and is indeed idolatrous because it places trust in an object rather than in [[God]]. The proper Christian defense against such spiritual attacks is prayer, the sign of the cross, Holy Water, and specifically, the prayer of [[Psalm 90 (91)]], which is an ancient exorcism prayer against demonic powers.

## Question 11: The Purpose of Catechism

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=01:08:52)

Another Ben, who is finishing his catechism class, asks about the true purpose of catechism. He contrasts the model of simply swapping "propositional realities" with what he intuits is a more formative purpose.

[Listen from here](https://media.ancientfaith.com/lordofspirits/los_2022-03-24.mp3#t=01:09:54)

[[Fr. Stephen]] explains that the modern idea of education as an "information dump" is flawed. Historically, education was about formation and apprenticeship. Catechism is not about passing a test on Orthodox facts; it is about *inculturation* into the life of the [[Orthodox Church]]. It is a process of formation and mentorship, teaching a person how to live as an Orthodox Christian, how to pray, fast, and participate in the life of the community. The godparent/sponsor relationship was originally a true mentorship, an apprenticeship in the Christian life. The goal is to become an adult Orthodox Christian capable of living out the faith.
