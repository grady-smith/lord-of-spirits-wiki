```markdown
## The History of Israel: Part Three

During the persecution under Antiochus IV Epiphanes, a new theology of martyrdom developed, exemplified by the Maccabean Martyrs (Eleazar, and a mother and her seven sons) as recorded in [[2 Maccabees]]. [Fr. Stephen explains that these martyrs understood their deaths not merely as unjust killings but as a voluntary self-offering](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:08:26) and a pleasing sacrifice to [[God]] on behalf of the sins of their people. Their obedience unto death was seen as an act of [[Atonement - Sacrifice]].

This concept becomes a crucial paradigm for the early Jewish Christians in understanding the death of [[The Messiah|Christ]]. His death was not just a tragedy but the ultimate fulfillment of this idea of the righteous one offering himself as a sacrifice for the sins of the people. This pre-existing theology allowed them to interpret the crucifixion in a way that was consistent with Jewish thought, rather than seeing it as a failure.
```

**[[Atonement]]_-_[[Sacrifice]].md**

```markdown
---
tags: [atonement, sacrifice, soteriology]

## The History of Israel: Part Three

The concept of atonement is significantly developed during the Maccabean period. [Fr. Stephen details how the Maccabean Martyrs offered their own lives and suffering as a "pleasing sacrifice to God" for the sins of the people](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:09:34). Their willing obedience to [[God]] even unto death, refusing to break the [[Torah]], was seen as an atoning act.

This establishes a precedent for understanding [[The Messiah|Christ's]] death as the ultimate atoning sacrifice. The early Jewish Christians interpreted the Crucifixion through this lens of martyrdom, seeing [[Jesus]] as the perfectly righteous one whose voluntary self-offering fulfilled this pattern of [[Salvation and Martyrdom]]. This theology is distinct from penal substitution, as it is about a pleasing, voluntary offering of obedience, not the absorption of divine punishment.
```

```markdown
## The History of Israel: Part Three

The [[Second Temple period]] saw significant developments and conflicts regarding the priesthood and the temple.

Stephen explains that this was seen as illegitimate by groups like the Pharisees because the Hasmoneans, while Levites, were not of the proper Zadokite lineage for the high priesthood, and kingship was prophesied to come from the tribe of Judah. This corruption of the priesthood was a major source of internal conflict.

**The Samaritan [[Temple]]:** [01:17:58](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:17:58) The Samaritans established their own temple on Mount Gerizim, which they believed was the true holy site designated by [[God]]. This temple was destroyed by the Hasmonean ruler [[John]] Hyrcanus, an act which cemented the deep animosity between Jews and Samaritans .

**The [[Temple]] in Leontopolis, [[Egypt]]:** [31:33](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=31:33) A group of Jewish exiles in [[Egypt]] built a "satellite temple" in the form of an obelisk. They did not see it as replacing the Jerusalem Temple but as an extension or "embassy" for day-to-day worship , while still sending tithes and making pilgrimages to Jerusalem. This temple existed until 73 AD.
```

```markdown
## The History of Israel: Part Three

The [[Second Temple period]] was characterized by diverse and evolving expectations of the Messiah.

**[[Cyrus the Great]]:** [37:25](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=37:25) The Hebrew Scriptures themselves refer to the Persian king [[Cyrus the Great]] as a "messiah" (anointed one). This is because God used him to liberate the Judahites from Babylon and authorize the rebuilding of the Temple , fulfilling a messianic function without being an Israelite.

**The Hasmonean Model:** [01:57:36](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:57:36) The military and political success of the [[Hasmonean Dynasty]] created a popular expectation for a messiah who would be a warrior-king. This "Hasmonean ideology" envisioned the Messiah as one who would conquer Israel's enemies by force and re-establish a sovereign, physical kingdom. This view was later adopted by groups like the Zealots.

**The Pharisaic Model:** [01:58:47](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:58:47) In contrast, the Pharisees and related movements held a different view. They believed that Israel's role was to faithfully keep the [[Torah]] and wait for [[God]] to intervene directly. Their messianic hope was not in a human military leader but in God's eschatological act to establish His kingdom , which would include the resurrection of the dead and the restoration of all Israel. This is the stream of thought that Christianity emerges from, with [[Jesus]] of Nazareth being identified as the Messiah who fulfills these promises.
```

```markdown
## The History of Israel: Part Three

In [[Romans]] 11, St. [[Paul]] uses the metaphor of an olive tree to explain the relationship between Israel and the Gentile believers in [[Christ]]. This analogy is a cornerstone of his theology regarding the restoration of Israel.

[Fr. Stephen explains that there is only one olive tree, which represents Israel](https://media.ancientfaith.com/audio/podcasts/lordofspirits/los_116.mp3#t=01:34:38). The root of this tree is [[Abraham]] and the faithful remnant of his descendants.
- **Natural Branches:** These are the Jewish people. Some "natural branches" are broken off due to their unbelief in [[The Messiah|Christ]]. However, St. [[Paul]] insists they can be "grafted in again" if they repent.
- **Wild Olive Shoots:** These are the Gentiles who come to faith in [[Christ]]. They are "grafted in" to the existing tree of Israel, and they derive their life and nourishment from its root. They do not replace the tree or become a new tree; they become part of the one people of [[God]].

This model refutes both supersessionism (the idea that the Church replaces Israel) and dispensationalism (the idea that [[God]] has separate, parallel plans for the Church and for ethnic Israel). The "fullness of the Gentiles" coming in is interpreted by St. [[Paul]] as the fulfillment of the prophecy of the restoration of the lost Northern Kingdom tribes, who were scattered among the nations. The final salvation of "all Israel" refers to the completion of this one tree, comprising the faithful remnant of Judah and the restored northern tribes (now represented by the Gentile believers).
```
